# FreightBridge constraint analysis

FreightBridge's scenario covers 85 refrigerated trucks, with possible expansion to 265 vehicles. Two-hour manual logs cannot support a 90-second response to refrigeration faults. We therefore place sensing, classification and durable incident storage on the truck. The operations centre receives alerts when communication permits. Our implementation represents one truck on a laptop; fleet costs are projections, and the operations backend is a separate local container [1].

Latency. A 1 degree Celsius per minute temperature rise makes the 90-second requirement operationally important. Connected-network delay alone does not disqualify cloud inference. TRAI's September 2025 Pune assessment reports operator median latencies of 14.1-54.0 ms; these city and surrounding-area measurements do not establish rural tail latency or cloud response guarantees [4]. Even a stated planning allowance of 500 ms network delay plus one second processing fits 90 seconds while connected. Coverage loss, queueing and tail behaviour are the decisive risks. Our controlled combined-fault run observed a local physical Critical alert at 35.08 seconds and a model Critical result at 40.16 seconds.

Bandwidth. Assuming float32 values, temperature requires four bytes per second and 500 Hz three-axis vibration requires 6,000. The numerical payload is 518.7456 decimal MB per truck daily, costing Rs 51.87456 at Rs 0.10/MB. With an explicit assumption of 100 door records daily at 32 bytes each, this becomes 518.7488 MB and Rs 51.87488. A planning example of 1,440 one-KB heartbeat messages plus 20 one-KB alerts uses 1.46 MB and Rs 0.146 daily, a 99.72% numerical-payload reduction. Message rates, encoding and protocol overhead must be measured in production. The simulator's 0.5 Hz vibration RMS is an already summarised stream, distinct from this raw 500 Hz business calculation.

Connectivity. A 35-90 minute outage lasts 2,100-5,400 seconds, approximately 23-60 times the entire alert budget. Local decisions and logging continue through these gaps. Remote receipt necessarily waits for connectivity. Seven queued events survived service recreation in our test and subsequently received acknowledgements. Privacy. Local inference reduces raw-data exposure, but contractual protection also needs authenticated access, encryption, secure updates and audit controls. Our private-network HTTP prototype does not demonstrate those production protections. In deployment, access control must cover the physical node, maintenance interface and backend, not only the radio link. Cargo identifiers and retained logs need explicit retention policies. Hash verification detects accidental artifact changes; it does not replace signed updates or authorisation.
